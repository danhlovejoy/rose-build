What Do Embeddings Encode?

Attributes and Analogy Math

An embedding model converts each word into a list of numbers, and those numbers encode the word's meaning.

1

Words as Vectors: Points in Space

Imagine describing every word with a few measurable attributes. Each attribute gets a number between −1 and +1, where +1 means the word has the attribute, −1 means it has the opposite (female instead of male, child instead of adult), and a value in between means the attribute partly applies. The result is a vector. It's a list of numbers that encodes what the word means.

With three attributes, each word becomes a point in 3D space. Drag the chart to rotate it. Six of the words are on the corners of a cube, where every value is −1 or +1. Prince and Princess are at −0.5 on Adult, halfway between 0 and child, because in everyday writing the words often describe young people (fairy tales, “little princess”) even though a real prince can be any age.

Zero Values: Not Every Attribute Applies

Attributes like Male and Adult are meaningful for people, but not for everything. Stage 1 used −1 to mean the opposite of an attribute. When a word has no connection to an attribute at all, that dimension is 0 instead, so Water gets 0 on Male because it is neither male nor female. The vector is still the same length, but some slots are empty.

Look at how many dimensions are colored for each word versus how many stay white. Dog and Puppy differ in one slot: Puppy is −1 on Adult, while Dog is 0 because “dog” doesn't specify an age.

This is what "sparse" means. When most values in a vector are zero, we call it sparse. TF-IDF vectors are sparse: thousands of dimensions, almost all zero. Embedding vectors are dense: hundreds of dimensions, almost none zero. A zero carries no information, so a sparse vector describes each word with only a few of its dimensions.

Toy Vectors and Real Embeddings

Our "toy" model above has 7 dimensions with human-readable labels. A real embedding model like all-MiniLM-L6-v2 produces vectors with 384 dimensions. Dimensions are not named. No single number means "royalty" or "gender." The meaning is distributed across all 384 values.

Real embeddings are dense. In our toy model, “Water” has 6 zero dimensions out of 7 (86% sparse). In the real all-MiniLM-L6-v2 vector for “water,” every dimension has a nonzero value. With 384 nonzero values, one vector can encode many relationships at once, including ones no human-labeled attribute covers. The trade-off is that you can no longer point to one dimension and say “that’s the royalty dimension.”
Negative values are normal. Our toy vectors already use them, and because each toy dimension has a name, you can read the sign: −1 on Male means female. The real vectors above range from about −0.17 to 0.25. Dimensions are not named, so a single negative value has no meaning by itself. Positive and negative values together define a direction in high-dimensional space, and the direction encodes the meaning, just as it did on the cube in Stage 1.
The analogy still works

The same King − Man + Woman ≈ Queen arithmetic works with trained embeddings. Mikolov et al. showed it in 2013 with Word2Vec vectors. Those vectors have hundreds of dimensions instead of three, so you can't plot them, but the operation is the same: subtract one vector, add another, and find the word with the highest cosine similarity to the result.

How Do Words Get Their Vectors?

No person chose the value 0.0476 for dimension 45 of “king.” A neural network computes every value during training on large amounts of text.

Words that appear in similar contexts get similar vectors. During training on billions of sentences, “king” and “queen” appear near the same surrounding words (“throne,” “crown,” “ruled”), so each training step moves their vectors a little closer together. “Water” appears in completely different contexts (“drink,” “river,” “flow”), so its vector ends up in a different region of the space.

The all-MiniLM-L6-v2 model you will use in the lab was trained on over one billion sentence pairs. The model card on Hugging Face (one of this week’s readings) lists the exact training datasets. After training, the 384 numbers for each word are fixed: you load the model, hand it a word or sentence, and it returns the vector. That is what you will do in Cell 1 of the lab.

Go deeper in the readings. The StatQuest video, Word Embedding and Word2Vec, walks through the training loop step by step. Jay Alammar’s The Illustrated Word2Vec shows the same process in diagrams: each training step moves the vectors of words that share contexts closer together and moves unrelated words apart.
