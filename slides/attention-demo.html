1

The Problem: Long-Distance Relationships

Read this sentence: “The trophy didn’t fit in the suitcase because it was too big.”

What does “it” refer to, the trophy or the suitcase? You resolve this by scanning backward through the sentence. Toggle the last word and the referent flips.

—
“it” → trophy
—
“it” → suitcase
Earlier sequence models had no direct way to do this. They processed words one at a time and passed a fixed-size state vector from each word to the next. When a pronoun appeared 7 tokens after its noun, as “it” does after “trophy” here, information about that noun had often been overwritten in the state.

2

Attention in Action

Attention removes that word-to-word relay. In a single pass, every word gets a relevance score against every other word in the sentence, and the scores are converted into attention weights. 3

Three Roles Every Word Plays

Each word’s embedding is multiplied by three weight matrices, and the results are three vectors with different jobs. Training sets the values in those matrices. Click each card to see what the role does.

Query (Q)

“What am I looking for?”

Key (K)

“What do I advertise?”

Value (V)

“What do I pass along?”

The query is the vector that gets compared against every key to produce scores. For “it,” you can read the query as encoding “I’m a pronoun; which noun do I refer to?”

The key is the vector that every query is compared against. The key for “trophy” might encode “singular noun that could be a referent.” A high dot product between a query and this key produces a high attention score.

The value is the vector that gets added into the output, scaled by the word’s attention weight. Queries and keys only set the weights. The values supply the content that gets combined.

attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(Q · KT) × V
Each role has its own weight matrix. Because the three projections are separate, training can set them independently. A word’s key does not have to match its own query, and its value can carry different information from either.
↓
4

The Arithmetic: Scores and Softmax Weights

Pick a word. Its query is multiplied (dot product) with the key of every word in the sentence, and each result is a raw score. Softmax converts the raw scores into percentages that sum to 100%, and each percentage sets how much that word’s value contributes to the output.

The weighted sum is the output. Each value vector is multiplied by its weight, and the results are added together. With “big,” 52% of the output for “it” comes from the value of “trophy” and 15% from “suitcase.” The other ten words, including “it” itself, supply the remaining 33%.

5

The Full Pipeline

Transformers stack dozens of attention layers and run multiple heads per layer. One layer runs these five steps in order.

1

Project into Q, K, V

Each word’s embedding (a single vector) is multiplied by three separate weight matrices (WQ, WK, WV), and the results are the query, key, and value vectors. Training sets the values in these matrices. A word like “it” ends up with a query that encodes “I need a noun referent,” while “trophy” gets a key that encodes “I am a referent.”

2

Compute compatibility scores

The current word’s query is multiplied (dot product) with every key in the sentence, including its own. A high dot product means a strong match. For “it” querying “trophy,” the score is high because the pronoun’s query aligns with the noun’s key.

3

Normalize with softmax

Softmax exponentiates each score and divides by the total, producing weights that are all positive and sum to 1.0. The highest score gets the largest weight, and the lowest gets a small weight that is never exactly zero.

4

Weighted sum of Values

Each value is scaled by its softmax weight and the results are summed. With “big,” the output for “it” takes 52% of its value from “trophy” and 15% from “suitcase,” with small fractions from the other words.

5

Repeat across heads and layers

Several heads run in parallel in each layer, and each head can track a different type of relationship, such as coreference or syntax. Their outputs are concatenated and passed to the next layer. The original 2017 Transformer used 8 heads per layer, and GPT-3 (175B parameters) uses 96 heads in each of its 96 layers. Stage 6 below shows four heads on this sentence.

Attention runs in parallel. Sequential models processed one word at a time, while attention computes the scores for every pair of words at once. Each step costs more compute, but there are far fewer sequential steps, which made it practical to train on text collections of hundreds of billions of tokens.
